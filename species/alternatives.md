# Alternatives where a tree does not fit, or where street-canyon pollution is a concern

All documents below were downloaded and READ in full text (not search snippets), access date 2026-11-21, unless marked "snippet". These are mostly northern-European studies (London, Guildford, Shanghai, Surrey modelling). None was done in Athens. Transfer to Athens is an assumption, flagged in section 5.

## 1. Key message for the tool

- In street canyons (buildings both sides), "high-level" vegetation (trees) generally worsens street-level exposure; "low-level" dense vegetation from ground to canopy top (hedges) generally improves it. Evidence base is small (4 hedge-in-canyon studies in the 2017 review).
- Spacing and crown size matter: wider tree spacing and smaller crowns reduce the penalty. The GLA says widely spaced trees "will have little effect on air quality" (neutral), so trees remain justified on shade/heat grounds.
- Where there is space, a hedge between traffic and pedestrians is the evidence-backed option. Where the canyon is deep (H/W > 2) the GLA does NOT recommend adding hedges.
- Athens-specific tension: the City Regulation (see planting_rules.md) bans planters in municipal green spaces (Art. 12 par.23) and abolishes pits on sidewalks up to 1.00 m (Art. 15 par.4); sidewalks of 2-2.5 m are common in the historic centre (CNN 2026). A hedge needs at least ~1.5 m thickness (Surrey toolkit below), which will often not fit on the pavement. A hedge or green screen would have to be a municipal design (Art. 10, 14) and respect the 1.50 m clear pedestrian path.

## 2. Abhijith, Kumar et al. 2017 (Atmospheric Environment)

Abhijith K.V., Kumar P., Gallagher J., McNabola A., Baldauf R., Pilla F., Broderick B., Di Sabatino S., Pulvirenti B. (2017). "Air pollution abatement performances of green infrastructure in open road and built-up street canyon environments - A review". Atmospheric Environment 162, 71-86. DOI 10.1016/j.atmosenv.2017.05.014.
Document READ: author accepted manuscript, https://researchrepository.ucd.ie/rest/bitstreams/26234/retrieve (HTTP 200, 52 pages). The publisher page (sciencedirect, S1352231017304855) returned HTTP 403. Page numbers below are the manuscript's.

Numeric findings (verbatim where quoted):
- Trees in canyons (Sec. 3.1.1, p.11): "an average increase of 26 to 96% in concentrations of different pollutants due to the presence of trees in street canyon compared to" a tree-free canyon (modelling and measurement studies listed in the review's Table 2). Conditions: result depends on aspect ratio, tree density (LAD), crown cover and wind direction (oblique wind critical).
- Hedges in canyons (Sec. 3.2, p.15-16): "Three of these studies observed that hedges reduced pollutant exposure by 24 to 61% at the footpath areas in street canyons". One study (Vos et al. 2013, ENVI-met) found an increase, and the review says to "generalise cautiously". Of the four hedge studies, three were modelling (Gromke 2016, Wania 2012, Vos 2013), one mixed measurement+modelling (Li et al. 2016, Shanghai).
- Optimum hedge height (p.16): "optimum height between 1 and 2m"; maximum reduction at breathing height along the footpath with a 2 m hedge in shallow canyons (H/W 0.18 and 0.4) and 1.1 m in a regular canyon (H/W 0.78); Gromke et al. (2016) found the maximum reduction with 2.5 m height in a shallow canyon (H/W 0.5). Gromke 2016 (Table 3, p.50): a central single hedge reduced area-averaged concentration by up to 61%, hedges on both sides by 39%; a discontinuous hedge (9 m gaps) showed the least reduction; higher and less permeable hedges reduced more.
- Li et al. 2016 measurement (Table 3): concentration reduction of 53% to 27% at 1.4 m and 36% to 24% at 1.6 m height (CO, Shanghai).
- Open road (Table 4, p.51-52): hawthorn hedge 2.2 m high, 1.6 m wide: 34% PM reduction (Tiwary 2008); hedges 1.7-2.4 m: hawthorn 66.2% and 83.5%, holly 58.3% and 76.1%, yew 17.5% and 20.5% (Tiwary 2005, the two numbers are the two particle metrics reported there); NO2 removal by evergreen vegetation 14-25% in January and 35-59% in July (Fantozzi 2015). Morakinyo et al. 2016 (conifer barrier) found INCREASES in PM2.5 behind the barrier in some wind conditions (about +25% and +18% for perpendicular and oblique wind). So results are mixed.
- Thickness (p.31-32): minimum vegetation thickness of 5 m and 10 m to remove about 50% of total suspended particles and 20 nm nanoparticles respectively (Islam 2012, Neft 2016, Shan 2007); "In excess of a 50% reduction was observed with a 10m thick green belt for numerous pollutants" (Conclusions, p.28). Those conditions are open roads with deep green belts, not Athens-type sidewalks.
- Conclusions (p.27-28): "In a street canyon environment, high-level green infrastructure (i.e. trees) generally has a negative impact on air quality while low-level dense vegetation with complete coverage from the ground to the top of the canopy (i.e. hedges) hinder the air flow underneath and hence generally show a positive impact." And: "Increasing the spacing between trees and reducing the cross-sectional area occupied by tree canopies (through increased pruning and selecting smaller trees) can usually reduce street level personal exposure". Evergreen species recommended for barriers in open-road conditions.

## 3. GLA 2019 (Greater London Authority)

"Using Green Infrastructure to Protect People from Air Pollution", Greater London Authority, May 2019, 22 pp. PDF READ in full: https://www.london.gov.uk/sites/default/files/green_infruture_air_pollution_may_19.pdf is a wrong URL (404); the working link is https://www.london.gov.uk/sites/default/files/green_infrastruture_air_pollution_may_19.pdf (HTTP 200; found via https://www.london.gov.uk/programmes-and-strategies/environment-and-climate-change/environment-publications/using-green-infrastructure-protect-people-air-pollution). Page numbers are printed page numbers.

- Mechanism (p.8-9, citing the 2018 Air Quality Expert Group report): the main value of vegetation is dispersion, not removal; deposition "typically removes just a few percent of PM and a similarly small fraction of NO2". "A vegetation barrier can as much as halve the levels of pollutants just behind the barrier"; the benefit "tails off with increasing distance from the hedge". King's College study: NO2 reduced by 23 per cent when a green wall was placed between a busy road and a school playground (Bowes Primary School, Enfield).
- Street canyons with busy traffic (p.10-11): "A dense avenue of trees could trap the pollution emitted from vehicles at street level"; "'dense avenue of trees' refers to trees packed so closely together that they form an almost unbroken canopy. Trees spaced more widely will have little effect on air quality". A hedge/vegetation barrier between road and pedestrians "may offer some protection"; effect "less certain and depends on local conditions". Limit: "we do not currently recommend adding vegetation barriers in canyons where the height of the buildings either side is more than twice the width of the street (height to width ratio > 2)".
- Quiet canyons (p.12): where street-level air is cleaner than above the roofs, a dense avenue of trees helps (blocks downward dispersion of polluted air).
- Open roads (p.15-16): hedge between cars and pedestrians works best bordering open space; closer to buildings "less certain". Rule of thumb: barrier height H protects up to (3H) - 3 m downwind; 2 m gives 3 m, 10 m gives 27 m (p.16). A tall barrier may increase exposure on the road side.
- Design (p.5, 19-20): hedges/green walls "extend from ground level to a height of at least 2m; and be as thick and dense as possible"; gaps reduce effectiveness but sight lines, vision splays and safety must be kept; evergreens preferred (deciduous "protection only when in leaf"); avoid invasive, poisonous and allergenic species; consult tree officers for species.
- Green walls (p.13, 17): "Green walls (if they are dense enough and well maintained) can be used instead of hedges as effective vegetation barriers between pedestrians and busy roads", particularly "where hedges and trees cannot be planted due to, e.g. sub-surface infrastructure constraints". Facade-mounted green walls in canyons: some potential via deposition only, "further research is needed". Green roofs: "limited potential" for street-level exposure (p.12, 17).
- Planting (p.18): VOC note: oaks are strong isoprene emitters (Quercus ilex is on the Athens list); mixing species mitigates.

## 4. University of Surrey / Kumar group

a) "Urban Greening 'How To' Toolkit: Urban Greening for Air Pollution Mitigation" (University of Surrey, Dec 2025), https://www.surrey.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2025-12/Urban-Greening-How-To-Digital-Toolkit-AIR-POLLUTION.pdf (HTTP 200, 8 pp, READ):
- "Hedges along busy roadsides could reduce local pollution by up to 63%" (Abhijith et al. 2019, open-road field study); Guildford study: "a reduction of 31% in ultrafine particles and of 52% in BC during peak traffic".
- "Green screens could reduce local pollution by 44%" (London schools); green gate: PM10 -32%, PM2.5 -19%.
- "Street trees have been shown to reduce PM concentrations by as much as 50% in open road conditions when no gaps or highly porous vegetation is present; however, trees with large spacing and trees in street canyons have shown the potential to increase local air pollution levels".
- Mixed trees plus hedges "up to 52%" in open-road conditions.
- Design for canyons: "continuous hedges with no spacing or gaps, have a height of ~2m and a minimum thickness of 1.5m"; hedges "close to the road or between the road and footpaths"; "in a shallow street canyon, a medium-sized and low density, highly porous canopy species may be suitable, whereas in a deep street canyon, a naturally compact tree or shrub may be more appropriate". Prefer low-VOC, non-invasive (native where possible), non-allergenic, non-toxic species.
- Tool mentioned: HedgeDATE (Barwise et al. 2021, Sustainable Cities and Society 75, 103299).

b) Kumar, Zavala-Reyes, Tomson, Kalaiarasan (2022), "Understanding the effects of roadside hedges on the horizontal and vertical distributions of air pollutants in street canyons", Environment International. READ only the Surrey press release https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/hedges-reduce-pollution-breathing-height-shallow-street-canyons-study-confirms (HTTP 200); the paper itself was NOT read. Press release: hedges "can help mitigate traffic-related pollution up to 1.7m"; six-week measurement at Du Cane Road, West London (a shallow canyon, one lane each way); the result depends on wind speed and direction, hedge characteristics and pollutant, "a need for more field studies".

## 5. Caveats and what is NOT sourced

- No Mediterranean or Athens study of hedges vs trees was found. Hot dry summers raise a conflict: hedges need irrigation, and Athens trees already receive City watering for 5 years (Athens Voice 2022). Hedge species suitable for Athens (e.g. Myrtus communis, Nerium oleander as used by the City; see species.csv) have NOT been tested for pollution capture in any document read.
- 2017 review: absolute % changes are scenario-specific, mostly CFD modelling, and one study contradicts. Do not present "24-61%" as a guaranteed effect.
- Not read: sciencedirect full text of Abhijith 2017 (403), Kumar 2022 paper itself, GLA's cited sub-studies, Air Quality Expert Group 2018 report, the 2019 Kumar "Implementing Green Infrastructure for Air Pollution Abatement" guide.
- Search snippet only (not read in the document): the Surrey result titles about open roads and Environment International DOI 10.1016/j.envint.2021.106883 listed by the search engine; not used for numbers.
